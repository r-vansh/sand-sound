# Sand obeys acceleration, not loudness

Real sand does not respond to sound as such. It responds to the acceleration of the surface under it, and it moves only where that surface's peak acceleration exceeds gravity (Γ = Aω²/g > 1). Above that threshold each grain bounces and random-walks with a step size set by the local vibration amplitude, so sand drains out of strongly shaking regions and piles up where the surface is quiet. That is the whole mechanism of a Chladni figure, and its continuum form is one equation: ∂ρ/∂t = ∇²(D(x)·ρ), with D zero wherever Γ < 1. A deeper bed behaves differently: it bounces as one body, heaps and convects just above 1 g, and from about Γ ≈ 2.4 forms millimetre-scale ripples that flip every drive cycle, but only at low frequencies with large strokes. For the browser simulation this means three linked parts: a bank of damped plate modes driven by the audio spectrum, a "shaking field" built from the energy-weighted sum of squared mode shapes, and a mass-conserving height field updated by that transport equation plus vibration-dependent slumping. Two honest consequences follow. Music produces weaker, blurrier figures than a sustained tone, and sand freezes the instant the sound stops. The physics of the threshold and the transport law is well sourced; the behaviour under music and every performance figure below are derivations or estimates, not measurements.

## Grains move only where the surface out-accelerates gravity

The control parameter is the dimensionless acceleration Γ = 4π²f²A/g. **For Γ > 1 the layer loses contact with the surface on each cycle and collides with it later**; below that it rides along ([Melo, Umbanhowar & Swinney 1995](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/patt-sol/9507003)). Working that formula backwards (my arithmetic from the cited definition), lift-off needs about 0.6 mm of stroke at 20 Hz, 25 µm at 100 Hz and roughly 1 µm at 440 Hz. At audio frequencies the plate looks perfectly still while the sand on it dances, which is why the simulation should never animate the plate itself.

Once grains bounce, they diffuse. Recent experiments describe them as random walkers whose diffusivity rises with local vibration amplitude and who gather where diffusivity is low ([Abramian et al. 2025](https://journals.aps.org/prresearch/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.7.L032001)). The follow-up work pins down the flux law, and this is the detail that most simulations get wrong: ordinary Fickian diffusion, j = −D∇ρ, predicts a uniform sand layer and no pattern at all, while the measured behaviour follows **j = −∇(Dρ), whose steady state is ρ ∝ 1/D** ([Devauchelle et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05930)). The same paper measures D rising roughly as the cube of grain velocity (exponent 3.3 ± 0.5) and states that below Γ = 1 "the grains do not leave the plate, and the diffusivity vanishes". The earlier Grabec model, a Gaussian hop with standard deviation proportional to local amplitude, gives D ∝ A² instead ([Grabec & Sok](https://www.dlib.si/stream/URN:NBN:SI:doc-UD1CHCV8/f3cc8dc7-ac00-40fb-a205-ee5fd5c6e100/PDF)). The two disagree on the exponent, so the practical form is D = D₀·max(0, Γ − 1)^p with p exposed as a knob between 2 and 3.

The hard cut-off at Γ = 1 does visual work. It gives each nodal line a finite width (the band where Γ < 1), and a louder drive narrows that band, so lines sharpen with volume as they do on a real plate. This line-width argument is reasoned from the transport law; the notes found no measured line widths or ridge heights.

Two inversions sit on top of this. Fine, light powder goes to the antinodes instead, carried by air currents the plate sets up; Faraday showed the effect depends on air, and it takes over when a grain's terminal fall speed drops to the plate's velocity, around **10–20 µm particles at 50 Hz** ([Aranson & Tsimring review](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0507419)). Separately, when the plate never exceeds 1 g, particles of any size drift to the antinodes even without air ([van Gerner et al. 2010](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/inversion-of-chladni-patterns-by-tuning-the-vibrational-accelerat/)). Ordinary sand in a hard-driven demo is firmly node-seeking, so both are optional extras: a "dust" toggle that reverses the drift would be physically grounded, not a gimmick.

Airborne sound by itself moves nothing. Acoustic forces lift even extremely light objects only from around 145 dB in a standing wave ([Frontiers for Young Minds](https://kids.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frym.2021.678021/full)). What works in every classic demonstration is sound driving a light, resonant surface: Jenny sang through a pipe onto a 60 cm drum membrane strewn with quartz sand, and low tones gave simple figures while high tones gave complex ones ([Cymatics source document](https://www.cymaticsource.com/_files/ugd/0e8d92_35728f46be7e420c97d4109ad8edc33d.pdf), a secondary and partly promotional source). The simulated "sand floor" is therefore best conceived as a plate or membrane that the audio excites, not as sand hit by sound.

## The pattern belongs to the plate, and frequency sets its grain

A metal plate obeys the fourth-order bending equation, not the membrane wave equation, and a square plate with free edges has no closed-form modes ([Porter 2017](https://people.maths.bris.ac.uk/~marp/abstracts/free-edge.pdf)). The consequence that matters visually is the dispersion: frequency scales with wavenumber squared, f = Ck²/2π, with C/2π ≈ 0.248 m²/s for 1 mm aluminium ([Tuan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8870825/)). **Pattern detail therefore grows as √f, not f**: quadrupling the pitch only doubles the number of nodal lines across the plate. From the cited constant, a 320 mm plate shows about 3 lines across at 250 Hz, 6–7 at 1 kHz and 13 at 4 kHz (my arithmetic). A membrane, by contrast, has f ∝ k, so its detail grows linearly with pitch. This choice is a single line of code and a real difference in feel.

For the shapes, the cheap and experimentally validated approximation is the cosine family cos(nπx/L)·cos(mπy/L) with k = (π/L)√(n² + m²), which Tuan's group used to reconstruct measured point-driven patterns ([Tuan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8870825/)). It is wrong for the lowest few modes: Porter's benchmark eigenvalues place the true first modes of the 320 mm plate near 34, 49 and 58 Hz, where the cosine model predicts 44–48 and 87–96 Hz (my arithmetic from both sources). The accurate alternative is Ritz's basis of free-bar functions, u_m(x)u_n(y) ± u_n(x)u_m(y), with published formulas and roots ([Gander & Wanner 2012](https://www.unige.ch/~gander/Preprints/Ritz.pdf)). For a visual experiment the cosine modes are the right starting point, with frequencies spaced as (n² + m²) and the base frequency exposed as a "plate size" knob.

Where the plate is driven decides which figures can ever appear. Centre-driven square plates show only four-fold symmetric patterns, and centre-driven circular plates only rings ([Lindemann & Vella, UIUC 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/T_Lindemann_A_Vella_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)). In the cosine model this follows from the drive coefficient cos(nπx′/L)·cos(mπy′/L): at the centre it vanishes unless n and m are both even, and degenerate pairs lock into the "+" combination. The popular web formula with a minus sign, used for instance by [luciopaiva/chladni](https://www.github.com/luciopaiva/chladni), produces exactly the family a centre driver cannot excite. That is fine as a look, but a movable drive point is the physically meaningful control: moving it off centre unlocks the asymmetric figures.

Each mode responds as a damped resonator. Tuan's fitted response weights every mode by its value at the driver times a complex Lorentzian, with damping γ = 0.16ω₀ ([Tuan et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6052176/)); from those fitted values the linewidth works out to about 5 Hz, or Q in the hundreds (my inference from one fit; no measured Q tables were found). Real resonances are correspondingly sharp, and demonstrators tune in 1–5 Hz steps to find a figure ([Rutgers physics demos](https://demos.physics.rutgers.edu/index.php?d=676)). The two source notes describe a frequency sweep differently and both are right. The mathematical nodal set deforms continuously between resonances, but the amplitude collapses there, Γ falls below 1, and the sand simply sits frozen in the old figure until the next resonance throws it apart and it re-condenses. On screen that reads as freeze, scatter, regather, not a smooth morph.

For several frequencies at once the notes found no experimental study, so this part rests on linearity alone. The response is linear in the source ([Tuan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8870825/)), so the time-averaged squared motion is the sum of each component's squared field. A point stays still only where every excited mode is near zero, which leaves nodal points and shallow valleys instead of lines. A sustained tone on a resonance draws a crisp figure; broadband music draws a soft, pulsing, slowly reorganising one. A faithful simulation will look calmer under music than single-tone demo videos suggest, and the Q knob is the honest way to trade realism for liveliness.

## A deeper bed ripples at half the drive frequency

A layer several grains deep stops acting as independent walkers and becomes one inelastic body. The classic regime map, measured on a 7-grain-deep layer at 67 Hz in vacuum, runs in order of drive strength ([Melo et al. 1995](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/patt-sol/9507003)).

| Γ range | What the bed does |
|---|---|
| below 1 | Rides with the plate; nothing visible |
| 1 to about 2.4 | Leaves the plate each cycle but stays flat |
| about 2.4 to 4 | Standing waves at f/2: squares at low frequency, stripes at high |
| about 4 | Hexagons |
| about 4.5 to 5.7 | Flat domains in opposite phase, separated by kinks |
| 5.7 to 7.6 | Patterns return at f/4 |
| above 7.6 | Disorder |

The f/2 response is the signature: every crest becomes a trough on the next cycle. The ripple wavelength follows **λ/H = 1.0 + 1.1·(f√(H/g))^−1.32**, with squares giving way to stripes at f ≈ 0.33√(g/H), and waves vanish for layers thinner than about two grains ([Umbanhowar & Swinney 2000](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/nlin/0001061)). For a 1.2 mm layer that gives ripples of roughly 3 mm at 67 Hz and 7 mm at 30 Hz (my arithmetic). Near wave onset the bed also supports oscillons, isolated peaks that become craters on alternate cycles ([Umbanhowar, Melo & Swinney 1996](https://www.nature.com/articles/382793a0)).

These patterns need Γ of 2.5 and up, which at 30 Hz means most of a millimetre of stroke. They are a subwoofer phenomenon; a 1 kHz tone cannot produce them. In open air, fine sand also heaps: heaps are formed and stabilised by airflow through the bed, and small heaps coarsen into one large one ([van Gerner et al. 2007](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/the-interplay-of-air-and-sand-faraday-heaping-unravelled/)). For the simulation this suggests a clean split by frequency band: bass energy drives bed ripples and heaping, mid and high energy drive Chladni-style migration.

The static rules complete the picture. Dry sand holds slopes up to about 34 degrees ([Wikipedia, Angle of repose](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_repose)). Under strong vibration a heap relaxes all the way to flat even when its slope is already below repose ([Tsuji, Otsuki & Katsuragi 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1708.03752)). That explains why Chladni ridges are low and rounded: their flanks sit where the plate still trembles and keep slumping, while the core at the node is at rest.

## The simulation is three coupled fields on one grid

**Audio to modes.** Keep 16–40 modes, each with a shape, a natural frequency and a Q. Per frame, weight the FFT power spectrum by each mode's Lorentzian and its coupling to the drive point, then ease each mode's energy toward that target so modes ring up and down instead of jumping. A truer alternative is one band-pass resonator per mode run on the raw signal, which resolves low modes better than FFT bins. This resonator-bank mapping is assembled from standard vibration theory; the notes found no existing open project that does it, and the Web Audio specifics were recalled from memory, not re-checked.

**Modes to a shaking field.** Compute S(x) = Σ Eₖ·wₖ·φₖ(x)², where Eₖ is mode energy and wₖ is an optional ω⁴ weight, because sand feels acceleration and high modes move it with far less displacement. Set Γ(x) = gain·√S, with automatic gain mapping the running peak to a chosen Γ_max so behaviour holds across input volumes. Degenerate mode pairs must be combined into one shape before squaring.

**Shaking field to sand.** The transport equation has an exact, cheap grid form. With λᵢ = Dᵢ·dt/dx², each cell sends the fraction λᵢ of its own sand to each of its four neighbours and receives λⱼρⱼ from each of them. Mass is conserved by construction and the steady state is ρ ∝ 1/D, matching the measured law. Keep λ at or below 0.2 and run several substeps per frame. Writing it as div(D∇ρ) instead silently produces a flat, patternless floor. These stability limits are the note-writer's own analysis and are untested.

Slumping uses the measured law for a vibrated pile: transport velocity proportional to plate velocity and to local sand depth, rising steeply as the slope nears repose, with a fitted efficiency **c = 0.068 ± 0.014** and onset between Γ = 1 and 2 ([Tsuji et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1708.03752)). It was validated on a uniformly vibrating plate, so applying it with a spatially varying field is an extrapolation. A cheaper stylised version is a talus rule whose threshold angle falls as local Γ rises; GPU terrain tools use the same structure with a threshold of tan θ = 0.57 ([Galin GPUThermalErosion](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Doc/Heightfield/class_g_p_u_thermal_erosion.html)). If visible avalanche fronts are wanted later, the two-layer BCRE model, a static height plus a rolling layer exchanging mass at the repose slope, is the next step up ([Prigozhin & Zaltzman](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0005239)).

**Where it runs.** WebGL2 with EXT_color_buffer_float makes R32F and RGBA32F textures renderable, which the ping-pong update requires ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/EXT_color_buffer_float)). Written in gather form, where each cell computes its own loss and its neighbours' gifts from the same function, the shader conserves mass. The one sourced CPU data point is discouraging for per-frame trigonometry: evaluating a pattern and gradient over about 500k cells took about 100 ms in plain JavaScript ([luciopaiva/chladni](https://www.github.com/luciopaiva/chladni)). Everything else about performance in the notes is an estimate; the working assumption is GPU for a 600–700 px grid with 8–16 substeps, CPU typed arrays for 512² with one to three.

**How it looks.** The best-documented sand shading is Journey's, as reconstructed by Alan Zucconi: a high-contrast diffuse term, saturate(4·dot(N·(1, 0.3, 1), L)), per-grain normal perturbation from a random-vector texture, and glitter from grains treated as tiny mirrors that sparkle only when their reflection aligns with the view ([Zucconi, diffuse](https://www.alanzucconi.com/?p=10052); [Zucconi, glitter](https://www.alanzucconi.com/?p=10059)). Numeric constants for the specular and glitter terms are not published. Two additions from the physics are worth making: shade shaken regions lighter and hazier, since real sand there is a blur of airborne grains, and let glitter twinkle only where Γ > 1, so stillness at the nodes reads as stillness.

## Conclusion

The research turns "sand reacting to sound" into something narrower and more buildable: sand reacting to a resonant surface, gated by a single threshold. That threshold supplies the tactile qualities for free. Sand has weight because nothing moves below 1 g. It settles because diffusivity vanishes at the nodes. It keeps its shape because friction returns the moment the drive stops, so a silent floor holds the last figure as a record of what was played. The implementation risk sits in the flux law and the gain staging, not in rendering.

The open questions are the ones the literature leaves open. Nobody has measured sand under music, line widths on real plates, or slumping in a spatially varying field, so those behaviours must be tuned by eye, and the right knobs are the physically meaningful ones: Q or sustain, Γ_max, the transport exponent p, plate size, drive point, and a node-or-antinode toggle for sand versus dust. The largest design decision is whether the piece is a thin layer on a plate or a deep bed on a woofer. The physics says they are different instruments that share a threshold, and a floor that does both needs bass and treble routed to different mechanisms.
